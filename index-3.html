The Molecules of Life
Carbohydrates Lipids Proteins Nucleic Acids
A field guide, in four specimens
by Quron Jordan

Everything you are is built from four small molecules.

Every bite, breath, and heartbeat traces back to carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids — the four organic compounds that build, fuel, and instruct every living cell. This is their story.

Specimen No. 1

Carbohydrates

The body's quick fuel — chains of sugar built for fast, ready energy and, in plants, sturdy structure.

C, H, O ratio approximately 1 : 2 : 1
Fig. 1 — a starch or cellulose chain, one glucose ring at a time

Types

  • Monosaccharides — single sugar units, the simplest form (glucose, fructose).
  • Disaccharides — two sugars bonded together (sucrose, lactose).
  • Polysaccharides — long chains of many sugars, built for storage or structure (starch, cellulose, glycogen).

Three examples

  • Table sugar
  • Bread & pasta (starch)
  • Cellulose in plant cell walls

In your life

The toast at breakfast and the rice at dinner are broken down into glucose, which your cells burn for nearly every quick task you do — thinking, sprinting, even just staying warm.

Without it

With no usable carbohydrates, the body shifts into breaking down fat and then muscle protein for fuel, leading to fatigue, low blood sugar, and — over time — muscle loss.

Cellulose, a carbohydrate, is the most abundant organic compound on Earth — it's the main material in wood and paper.
Your brain runs almost entirely on glucose, using roughly 120 grams of it a day even though it's only about 2% of your body weight.
Specimen No. 2

Lipids

Dense, water-repelling molecules that store energy, wall off every cell, and carry the body's hormone signals.

C, H, O far less oxygen than carbohydrates
Fig. 2 — heads face water, tails hide from it

Types

  • True fats (triglycerides) — long-term energy storage (butter, body fat).
  • Phospholipids — form the double-layered wall of every cell membrane.
  • Steroids — ring-shaped lipids that act as hormones and structural molecules (cholesterol, estrogen, testosterone).

Three examples

  • Olive oil
  • Cholesterol
  • Cell membrane phospholipids

In your life

Fat under your skin keeps you warm, fat around organs cushions them, and the fat in your diet lets you absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K — none of which dissolve in water alone.

Without it

Without lipids, cells have no membranes to hold together, fat-soluble vitamins can't be absorbed, hormone production stalls, and the body loses its insulation and energy reserves.

Gram for gram, fat holds more than double the energy of carbohydrates or protein — about 9 calories per gram versus 4.
Phospholipids arrange themselves into a bilayer automatically in water — no instructions needed, just chemistry.
Specimen No. 3

Proteins

Folded chains of amino acids that build tissue, carry cargo, and run nearly every chemical reaction in the body.

C, H, O, N often with a trace of sulfur
Fig. 3 — beads on a string, folded into shape by chemistry

Types

  • Structural — build tissue and hold shape (keratin, collagen).
  • Transport — carry substances through the body (hemoglobin carries oxygen).
  • Regulatory — enzymes and hormones that control reactions (amylase, insulin).

Three examples

  • Keratin in hair & nails
  • Hemoglobin
  • Digestive enzymes

In your life

The chicken or beans on your plate supply amino acids that rebuild muscle after exercise, grow your hair and nails, and arm your immune system with antibodies.

Without it

Without enough protein, muscles waste away, wounds heal slowly, the immune system weakens, and severe deficiency can cause the swelling and stunted growth seen in kwashiorkor.

Only 20 amino acids exist in the human body, yet their combinations build tens of thousands of different proteins.
Collagen, a structural protein, makes up roughly a third of all the protein in your body — more than any other kind.
Specimen No. 4

Nucleic Acids

The instruction library of the cell — molecules that store and carry out every genetic direction a body follows.

C, H, O, N, P phosphorus sets this molecule apart
Fig. 4 — a twisted ladder of genetic code

Types

  • DNA — deoxyribonucleic acid, the permanent, double-stranded blueprint stored in the nucleus.
  • RNA — ribonucleic acid, a single-stranded working copy that carries out DNA's instructions.

Three examples

  • DNA in your chromosomes
  • Messenger RNA (mRNA)
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA)

In your life

Your DNA is why you have your eye color, your height range, and even a tendency toward certain talents or health risks — it's the instruction manual every one of your cells reads from.

Without it

Without functioning nucleic acids, cells cannot build proteins or copy themselves, which makes life itself impossible — and damaged nucleic acids are the root cause of genetic disorders and many cancers.

Uncoiled, the DNA packed into just one of your cells would stretch about two meters long.
Humans share roughly 60% of their DNA with a banana — a reminder that all life reads the same genetic code.
